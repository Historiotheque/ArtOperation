# 06 · Continuity

Of everything in this manual, one thing is **primordial**: maintaining operational continuity. Not talent. Not inspiration. Not the quality of any single piece. *Continuity.* The unbroken succession of sessions, each one inheriting the last and bequeathing to the next. Everything else in the operation serves this; nothing in the operation outranks it.

## What continuity is

Continuity is the operation *staying alive*. A living operation has a heartbeat — the regular session, the daily opening of the box — and as long as the heartbeat continues, the operation lives, learns, and compounds. Pause the heartbeat long enough and the operation dies: not dramatically, but quietly, the way an unattended garden goes to weed. Nobody announces the death. You simply notice, one day, that you "used to" run an operation.

Continuity is therefore not a feeling of being "on a roll." Rolls end. Continuity is structural: the log is unbroken, the series advances, the workspace is resumable, the next step is always known. It is the least glamorous virtue in this manual and the only indispensable one.

## What breaks it

Know the enemies, because they never announce themselves as enemies:

- **The unlogged absence.** You miss sessions and don't record the missing. The log develops amnesia about its own gaps, and the gaps widen — because a gap the record doesn't acknowledge is a gap that doesn't hurt, and what doesn't hurt doesn't get repaired.
- **Trace loss.** Materials vanish, files corrupt, the notebook from March is nowhere. Every lost trace is a piece of the operation's memory gone, and memory does not regenerate.
- **Momentum death.** The series stalls — not from a decision, but from drift. No one declared it over; it simply stopped being worked on. Stalled series rarely restart, because restarting requires reconstructing the momentum that continuity would have preserved for free.
- **The unresumable workspace.** You leave the studio in a state no one — including you — can resume from. The next session begins with excavation instead of work, and excavation is where good intentions go to die.

None of these feels fatal in the moment. That is what makes them fatal.

## Savepoints

The practical mechanic of continuity is the **savepoint**: end every session by leaving the workspace — and the record — so that the operation can be resumed cold, by a stranger, tomorrow morning. That stranger is you, after a bad night's sleep.

A proper savepoint has four parts:

1. **The next step, written down.** Not "continue with the series" — the *actual* next physical action: "glue the third collage element on panel four." Vague next steps are where resumption goes to die.
2. **Materials laid out.** Whatever the next step needs is staged and visible. The future operator should not have to hunt.
3. **The log closed.** The session entry is finished, dated, and honest — including what went wrong. An unclosed log is an open wound in the record.
4. **The workspace surveyed.** One look around: everything in its home, nothing perishable left out, the state of the room readable at a glance.

A savepoint takes ten minutes. It is the highest-leverage ten minutes in the operation.

## The tax on forced backtracking

When continuity breaks, you pay to reconstruct what continuity would have preserved for free — re-learning what you knew, re-finding what you had, re-deciding what was decided. Call it **the tax on forced backtracking**, and understand that it compounds: a week's break costs more than a week's work to repair; a month's break can cost a season. The operation doesn't just pause during the gap — it *regresses*, because the thread of the series, the feel of the technique, the thread of thought all decay without use.

This is why continuity outranks intensity. The heroic all-night session followed by three silent weeks nets less than the modest daily session that never breaks. Steady beats heroic, always, because steady compounds and heroic merely spikes.

## Don't break the chain

The rule is simple: **don't break the chain.** Every day gets its session — and here is the part most people miss: the session has a *minimum*. Define yours: twenty minutes, one log entry, one small physical act of the operation. On the worst days — illness, grief, exhaustion — you do the minimum, you log it honestly, and the chain holds. A minimum session is not a failed session; it is a successful act of continuity. Maintenance days count. Sick days with a log entry count. The chain does not grade your performance; it only records your presence.

What the chain cannot survive is the silent zero — the day nothing happened and nothing was recorded. Zeroes compound faster than anything else in this manual. Never let a zero go unlogged: if the operation truly stopped for a day, *write that down*, with the reason. A recorded zero is a fact the operation can work with. An unrecorded zero is the beginning of amnesia.

## What to do today

1. **Establish the savepoint ritual.** Write down your four-part version (next step, materials, log, survey) and perform it at the end of today's session. Time it. It should take ten minutes.
2. **Define your minimum session.** The smallest unit that keeps the chain alive on your worst day. Write it in the Master Plan (Chapter 05).
3. **Audit the chain.** Look at your log as a calendar: are there unlogged zeroes? Repair them honestly — backfill what you remember, mark what you don't, and note the repair itself. Then resolve: no more silent zeroes.

Continuity is primordial. Everything else is commentary.
