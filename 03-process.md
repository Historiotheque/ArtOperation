# 03 · Process

Preparations are complete (Chapter 02). Now the operation runs. The process is simple — not easy, simple. It is an ordered procedure, the same every time, and its simplicity is what makes it survive contact with real life.

Understand what you are running: **the Art Operation is an algorithmic process, performed by humans, as a manual step-by-step manufacturing operation.** An algorithm does not improvise; it executes. Your job as operator is to design the procedure once, carefully — and then to execute it faithfully, session after session, while the procedure does the heavy lifting of keeping you honest. Inspiration is welcome. It is not load-bearing.

## The procedure, in order

### 1. Lock the technique

Stick to your *technique*, to your *design methodology* — for the duration of the series, without deviation. The technique is the constant; everything else is the variable. An operation that changes its technique every session is not experimenting; it is flailing, and it will never learn what any single technique can actually do. Lock it, work it to exhaustion, and only then — between series, never inside one — consider changing it.

### 2. Define the series

Work in *series*, building *collections*. A series is not "some works that look alike." It is a defined population with membership criteria: what counts as a member, what the collection target is (twelve pieces, fifty, a hundred), and what question the series is asking. Define this *before* the first piece, in the log. A series without a definition is a pile; a series with one is an experiment.

### 3. Run the session

The workspace is where everything happens — your Art-House and your Archives in one place, your Historiotheque, distributed over time and space: take your time, find your place. Enter it as Chapter 01 taught you, and work.

**Document your practice as you go.** Narrate your work to yourself out loud. Take audio or video notes — lifelogging, or whatever method keeps you on top of your game. The narration is not commentary for posterity; it is the operator thinking in real time, captured before it evaporates. The session that is not narrated is a session half-remembered, and half-remembered sessions cannot be iterated on. (The full doctrine is Chapter 04; the habit starts here.)

### 4. Go into the field

**Experiment in the laboratory that is the world.** Go outside. Go for a soundwalk. Practice psychogeography, the dérive — the disciplined art of getting productively lost. Collect data *In The Field*: recordings, photographs, notes, found objects, overheard sentences. Remember that out there you are the mobile Historiotheque (Chapter 01) — the witness-box does not stop testifying when it leaves the building.

Fieldwork is not a break from the operation. It is the operation's intake phase, and it is scheduled like everything else.

### 5. Manufacture in the lab

Bring the collected data back to the atelier and **manufacture cultural artifacts**. That verb is deliberate: *manufacture*. The lab phase is production — the field's raw intake transformed, through your locked technique, into members of the series. This is bench work. It is repetitive by design. The romance of the field and the discipline of the bench are the two strokes of the engine; an operation needs both, in alternation, indefinitely.

*See also: the Switchboard Method — the daily rhythm of the process, run in alternation.*

### 6. Measure against the real world

Find what is most relevant — and **iterate continuously**. Measurement here does not mean sales figures or like counts. It means: put the work in front of its implied audience (more below), compare the artifact against the series' defining question, and note the delta between what you intended and what happened. Then adjust the *next* iteration, never the current piece mid-stream. The series learns; the individual piece is already history.

### 7. Generate populations

Do not make *a* work. **Generate populations of works.** This is best done through the science, methods, and theories behind your operation — but the practice is simple: produce variants, keep the variants, and let the series exert selection pressure across them. Ten studies of the same problem teach more than one masterpiece and nine abandoned attempts, because the ten can be compared and the one cannot. Populations are data; masterpieces are anecdotes. A mature operation runs on data.

### 8. Back everything up into the Refcards-System

Close the loop: **use the Refcards-System as work-system.** Document the session and back everything up into it — the cards, the log, the variants, the measurements. The operation's memory is not in your head; it is in the system, where it survives bad moods, bad months, and bad years. A session that is not backed up into the system did not happen, as far as the operation is concerned. The archive you prepared in Chapter 02 is fed here, every session, without exception.

## A note on audience

**Monitor your audience** — your *implied* audience, the one the work is for, whether or not they have arrived yet. Experiment with different ways of reaching them, and with different media and documentation *for* them: the same series can be presented as prints, as a feed, as a talk, as a dataset. The work does not change; the interface does. Find what is most relevant by testing, not by theorizing. The audience is part of the real world you measure against — treat them as an instrument reading, not as a verdict on your worth.

## What the process guarantees — and what it doesn't

Followed faithfully, this procedure guarantees *output*: a growing, documented, iterated body of work, produced continuously, with its memory intact. It guarantees that you will know what you did, why you did it, and what happened next. That is an enormous guarantee — most artistic lives cannot claim it.

It does not guarantee recognition, income, or understanding. Those belong to the world, not to the procedure. The procedure's promise ends at the edge of your operation; what the world does with your output is the world's business. Chapter 09 says more about this. For now: run the procedure, close the loop, and let the populations accumulate. The operation compounds.
