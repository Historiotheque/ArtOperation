# 07 · Practice

Process (Chapter 03) runs the work. *Practice* renews the worker. An operation that only produces will eventually produce with a dull instrument — the technique stiffens, the curiosity thins, the operator coasts on what worked five years ago. Practice is the chapter where you deliberately stay sharp: rehearsal, experiment, and learning, performed as rigorously as the production itself. The operation compounds (Chapter 03); practice is what keeps the compounding from compounding your mistakes.

## Rehearsal

**Continuously rehearse, continuously practice what you have learned.** Every discipline has its scales — the pianist's arpeggios, the painter's studies, the writer's exercises — and the mature operator practices them on purpose, not just when a piece demands it. Rehearsal is production with the pressure removed: the technique exercised for its own sake, so that when the series needs it, it is there without thinking.

Distinguish rehearsal from performance. The session has both. Performance is the series advancing, the artifacts accumulating. Rehearsal is the ten studies that will never be shown, the soundwalk with no recording obligation, the card written to test an idea rather than to keep it. Schedule rehearsal explicitly — a standing portion of the week — or the pressure of production will eat it, and the technique will quietly go stale underneath you.

## Experiment

**Learn to experiment** — as a discipline, not as an excuse. There is a world of difference between the operator who experiments and the dilettante who flails, and the difference is *protocol*:

1. **Bound it.** An experiment has edges: this material, this week, this question. Unbounded experimenting is just working without a series definition (Chapter 03).
2. **Hypothesize.** State what you expect to happen, in the log, before you begin. The hypothesis will often be wrong; that is its job.
3. **Keep it small.** Experiments are cheap by design. If the experiment requires the full resources of a production series, it isn't an experiment — it's a gamble.
4. **Log the result.** Especially the negative ones. A failed experiment, honestly recorded, is a permanent asset of the operation. An unrecorded one is a rumor you'll have to re-test in two years.

Apply the rigorous, experimental, iterated, evolutionary workflow: generate variants, let the work exert selection pressure, keep what survives, iterate. Evolution does not flail either — it varies *within* a protocol and selects *against* a criterion. Be the evolution, not the weather.

Note the relationship with Chapter 03's locked technique: experiments live in *designated* space — rehearsal sessions, bounded trials — not inside a production series mid-run. The series holds its technique; the laboratory holds the experiments; the log holds both. Discipline in production, freedom in the lab, honesty in the record.

## Learn to learn

**Learn to learn: your survival as an artist depends on it.** Techniques age. Media mutate. The discipline you mastered at thirty will not be the discipline the world asks for at fifty — and the operator who stopped learning at thirty-five is running a museum of their own past, not an operation.

This is not motivational decoration; it is cognitive maintenance. You need to deliberately build back up the **fluid intelligence** you had at fifteen to thirty-five — the raw capacity to absorb the unfamiliar, to hold new structures in mind, to learn fast. Fluid intelligence atrophies without use and rebuilds with it. Study outside your discipline: the painter reads logistics theory, the musician studies archival science, the novelist learns a programming language. The unfamiliar is resistance training for the mind (Chapter 02), and the operation feeds on what the learning brings back.

Thirty years in, I am still a student of my own operation. That is not false modesty; it is the job description. The day the operator stops learning, the operation starts dying — slowly, then all at once.

## Document like your life depends on it

**Continuously document everything. Document like your LIFE DEPENDS ON IT — because it does.** Practice without a record is exercise without a training log: the body may benefit, but the operation learns nothing. Log the rehearsals, the experiments, the hypotheses and their outcomes, the things you are learning and how fast. The practice log is where the operator's growth becomes visible — and visible growth is what turns thirty years of sessions into thirty years of *development* rather than thirty years of repetition.

And maintaining operational continuity is **primordial** (Chapter 06) — practice included. The rehearsal skipped without a log entry, the experiment abandoned mid-protocol: these are continuity breaks wearing a lab coat. The chain holds for practice days too. Minimum sessions apply to the lab as well as the bench.

## What to do today

1. **Schedule rehearsal.** Carve a standing portion of this week for technique exercised without production pressure. Put it in the Master Plan (Chapter 05).
2. **Design one small experiment.** Bounded, hypothesized, cheap, this week. Write the hypothesis in the log before you begin.
3. **Name one thing to learn this month** — outside your discipline, deliberately unfamiliar. Write down what it is and how you'll study it.
4. **Log all of it.** The rehearsal, the experiment, the learning. The practice that isn't recorded didn't happen, as far as the operation is concerned.

Sharp tools, tested methods, a learning operator. That is what practice buys.
