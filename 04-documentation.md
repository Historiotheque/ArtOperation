# 04 · Documentation

One must be *diligent* about documentation. One must be careful to document, and to document everything carefully. This is not the boring part of the operation that you do after the real work. The documentation process is one of the most important parts of running a successful Art Operation — and I will go further: **documentation alone can ensure you preserve operational continuity at all times.** Everything else in this manual can fail for a week and the operation survives. If the documentation fails, the operation is already over; it just doesn't know it yet.

## What gets documented

Not the finished works. The works document themselves. What gets documented is everything *around* the works — the part that evaporates:

- **Decisions.** What you decided, when, and why. The technique you locked, the series you defined, the venue you chose. A decision undocumented is a decision you'll re-litigate every month.
- **Failures.** Especially failures. The piece that collapsed, the experiment that produced nothing, the day the procedure broke down. Failures are data (Chapter 05), and data not recorded is data destroyed.
- **Deltas.** What changed between sessions — in the workspace, in the work, in your understanding. The operation learns through deltas; undocumented change is change that teaches nothing.
- **Consultations.** What you asked the archive, and what it answered (Chapter 02). The operation's memory must record its own use.
- **Measurements.** What the real world said back (Chapter 03). Intention versus result, logged plainly, without flattery.

Notice what is *not* on the list: justifications, press releases, and narratives about what the work means. Documentation is testimony, not publicity. The witness-box testifies truthfully (Chapter 01) — and testimony that flatters is perjury.

## Continuity lives in the record

Here is the mechanism behind the claim above: operational continuity does not live in your head. It lives in the record. Your memory is lossy, self-serving, and perishable; the record is none of those things, provided you keep it diligently. When you return to the workspace after a month away — illness, grief, a dead hard drive, life — the record is what lets you resume *exactly* where you left off: what the series was asking, what the last session concluded, what the next step was supposed to be. Without it, you resume approximately, which is to say you start over. **Continuity is a property of the documentation, not of the operator.** The operator is mortal and forgetful. The record, kept properly, is neither.

## The public audit trail

Your documentation must live in a **public-facing venue** — a repository anyone can access, from anywhere in the world. Not on a single laptop. Not in a drawer. In the light.

This is not exhibitionism; it is engineering. A private log can be edited, backdated, or quietly abandoned without anyone noticing — including you. A public log is *witnessed*, and a witnessed record behaves differently: it disciplines the entries (you write more honestly when the page is public), it survives your hardware (the venue outlives the laptop), and it constitutes an **audit trail** — a complete, timestamped, inspectable history of the practice. Anyone, at any time, can verify what the operation did and when. That verifiability is not a bonus feature. It is the entire point of calling the studio a Witness-Box.

## The civic virtues

A public audit trail exists so that the practice can uphold the civic virtues — and I mean them operationally, not decoratively:

- **Integrity** — the record says what happened, not what would look good.
- **Transparency** — the procedure is visible; there is no hidden step, no secret method.
- **Responsibility** — every entry has an author, and the author is you. The operation answers for itself.
- **Truth** — the log is corrected, never rewritten. Errors are struck through and annotated, not deleted. (More on this in Chapter 05.)
- **Accountability** — because the trail is public, the operation can be checked — by your future self, by peers, by history. Welcome the checking; it is the point.

These are not morals I am imposing on art. They are the load-bearing properties of a record anyone can trust — including you, in five years, when you need to know what you actually did.

## Work in the light

Learn to work **in the light**. Nature abhors darkness — and *working in the dark* causes **suspicion** to arise. Besides losing operational continuity, the second-worst thing that can happen to a practice is to arouse suspicion about it: the undocumented claim, the unverifiable achievement, the work with no trail. Suspicion, once aroused, is almost impossible to dispel, because the only cure for it is the very record that doesn't exist.

Working in the light is therefore not a moral posture. It is risk management. Every session logged publicly is a session no one — including your own doubting future self — can question.

## The rule

**When in doubt, refer back to the repository and to your notes.** Lost in a series? Read the log. Can't remember why you chose a technique? The decision is recorded. Suspect your memory of flattering you? Check the measurements. The documentation is the operation's ground truth, and ground truth is what you stand on when everything else shifts.

## What to do today

1. **Establish the public venue.** A repository, a site, a notebook system with public copies — somewhere anyone can access, that is not your laptop alone. Today.
2. **Fix the log format.** Decide what a session entry contains (date, series, technique, decisions, deltas, measurements) and write the template down. Every entry follows the template.
3. **Log today's session** — including this chapter. What you decided while reading it counts as a decision.
4. **Back it up.** The venue is one copy; make the others (Chapter 02). A public record with no redundancy is a public risk.

Document like your life depends on it. Chapter 07 will tell you: it does.
