# 02 · Preparations

Like in any venture or undertaking, the first step of the journey is always a preparation process. The box is open (Chapter 01). Before you touch a material or run a procedure, you prepare — completely, without shortcuts, with a good conscience.

## The road-trip rule

Imagine going on a road trip — a long one, across the country. Do you not make all the necessary preparations? You pack the lunch. You lock the house and make sure nothing is left running that shouldn't be running. The gas tank is full. The bags hold everything you could possibly need. You carry a certain amount of cash in case the electronic banking fails you somewhere remote. The vehicle is in working order, and a dozen smaller things besides. The point is: you checked, and double-checked, and triple-checked absolutely *everything* — or else you cannot leave with a **good conscience**.

Why would anyone set out on an Art Operation journey without proper preparations? It is the same road, and the same rule applies. **Always prepare beforehand.** What follows are the six preparations, in order. Do not skip one because it bores you; the one you skip is the one that will fail you mid-journey.

## 1. Prepare the workspace

The workspace is where everything happens (Chapter 08 gives it its full treatment), so it is prepared first. A prepared workspace is *surveyable*: you can see, at a glance, what is where, what is in progress, and what is missing. Every tool has a home. Every surface has a function. Nothing you need is buried; nothing you don't need is in the way.

Prepare it the night before if you can. The workspace you prepare today is the workspace you inherit tomorrow morning — and the operator who inherits a prepared workspace starts working immediately, while the one who inherits a mess spends the first hour excavating. That hour, multiplied across a career, is the difference between an operation and a hobby.

## 2. Prepare yourself

Prepare yourself mentally to attack *aesthetic problems* with your arsenal of *techniques and methods*. Name the problem you are going to work on — out loud, in the log, in one sentence. Survey your arsenal: which techniques are sharp right now, which are dull, which does this problem actually require? Choose the day's technique deliberately, the way you would choose the right vehicle for the road trip. The wrong technique applied with great energy is still the wrong technique.

Readiness is also a matter of honesty: know what state you are in and plan accordingly. An operation run by a human must account for the human. Some days are for the hardest problems; some days are for maintenance, correspondence, and filing. Both are operational days. Only the unexamined day is wasted.

## 3. Prepare the raw materials

The British writer and designer William Morris is purported to have said: *"You can't have art without resistance in the materials."* For our purposes, let us simply say that resistance **is** in the materials — and leave the philosophical diatribe for another book.

Inventory what your discipline consumes. Paint, brushes, collage elements, canvas, adhesive; or disk space, software, instrument strings, film stock; or paper, pens, index cards. **All raw materials are inputs in the process of building a mature Art Operation** — and no inputs means no operation. Stock the substrates *before* you begin. Running out mid-series is not bad luck; it is a preparation failure, and preparation failures are the only kind this chapter exists to prevent.

And here is the box-maker's lesson, learned the hard way: the original Art-Ops-In-A-Box held enough raw materials to *start* — paints and brushes for a painter. That was sufficient for a box and insufficient for an operation. A mature operation learns to source its own inputs, continuously, as a standing procedure rather than a periodic emergency. Know your suppliers. Know your consumption rate. Never discover mid-work that the well is dry.

## 4. Prepare the reference materials

Stand up the Refcards-System and the archive from day one — not "once the operation is running," because the operation is never more in need of its memory than at the beginning. One concept per card, numbered, cross-referenced: the atomic knowledge base on which everything else rests.

As you document your practice, keep good references. Log absolutely everything, and keep your **refmats ready-to-hand** — your refmats are your friends, keep them close. Source material is not mere *inspiration*; it is the **spiritual and moral substrate** of the work: your references in the history of arts and culture, in our shared documentary heritage. Art cannot exist without references, exactly as academic research cannot exist without citations. These references form an intricate network inside the operation. Tend it like a garden, not like a junk drawer.

*See also: the Refcards-System repository — the reference materials as a working system.*

## 5. Document and consult the archives

Before making anything new, consult what the operation already knows. The archive is a colleague: ask it what was tried, what failed, what worked, and what was left unfinished. Then **log the consultation** — the fact that you looked, what you looked for, and what you found. An operation that does not consult its archive repeats its own past at full price, paying for lessons it already bought.

This is also where redundancy begins: have multiple copies of *everything*, and keep logs in more than one place. Redundancy is not paranoia; it is the engineering answer to *archival collapse* — the slow, quiet failure by which an operation's memory rots from the inside. A log that exists in one place is a rumor. A log that exists in three places is a record.

## 6. Prepare for publication

Decide the work's destination *before the work exists*. This is the preparation most artists skip and the one they most regret skipping: the piece is finished, it is good, and then it sits on a hard drive for six years because no venue, no identifier, no filename convention, and no public record was ever prepared for it.

Publication preparation means: the naming scheme is decided (filenames that sort, identify, and survive), the identifiers are ready (the work will be findable and citable from birth, not as an afterthought), and the audit trail has a public venue waiting (a repository anyone can access — the civic virtues of Chapter 04 begin here, at preparation, not at release). The Verifiability Stack documents this doctrine in full; for now, the rule is simple: **nothing enters the operation without knowing where it will be published.**

## The road-trip checklist

You cannot leave with a good conscience until every box is checked:

- [ ] The workspace is prepared, surveyable, and inherited ready-to-work
- [ ] You are prepared — the problem named, the technique chosen deliberately
- [ ] Raw materials inventoried, stocked, and re-sourcable
- [ ] Reference materials ready-to-hand — Refcards-System and archive stood up
- [ ] Archives consulted before new work; the consultation logged; redundancy in place
- [ ] Publication prepared — destination decided, naming scheme ready, audit trail waiting

Check it the way you checked the gas tank. Then go.
